passburn

How it works — and why we can't read your secrets.

Encrypted before it leaves your browser

When you create a link, your browser generates two independent random values: a public part and a private part. Your secret is encrypted right there in the page with AES-256-GCM, using a key derived (HKDF-SHA-256) from both parts together. Only the resulting ciphertext and the private part are sent to our server.

Half the key never reaches us

The public part goes into the link after the # symbol — the URL fragment. Browsers never transmit fragments to servers, so that half of the key exists only in the link itself. This means:

· Someone who steals our database can't decrypt anything — they don't have the fragment.
· Someone who intercepts the link can't get the data from us after it's been viewed — it's already deleted.
· We can't read your secret, ever. There is nothing to subpoena, leak, or lose.

Burns after reading

Opening the page does not consume the secret — email scanners and chat apps that pre-fetch links only ever see the confirmation screen. The secret is released once, when the recipient explicitly clicks Reveal, and the server deletes its copy in the same operation. Every secret also has a hard expiry, whether or not it was viewed.

Live mode: never stored at all

A live link goes one step further — the server never holds the secret, not even encrypted. The secret stays in your open browser tab. When the recipient opens the link and knocks, you see the request in real time (with its approximate location) and choose to approve or deny. On approval, the encrypted payload relays through the server directly between the two browsers and the link burns the moment the recipient confirms delivery. The server sees only ciphertext passing through; close your tab and the link is simply dead.

Optional password, done right

If you set a password, it's stretched with PBKDF2 (600,000 iterations) into two independent values: one is mixed into the encryption key, the other acts as a check so a wrong guess doesn't consume the one allowed view. The password itself is never sent to us — share it over a different channel than the link.

Guessing is capped. After 25 wrong attempts the secret destroys itself and the sender is told, because anyone guessing passwords at a link already has the link — at that point the safe outcome is a dead secret and a warned sender, not a longer guessing game.

Post-quantum safe by design

passburn uses only symmetric cryptography — there is no key exchange and no public-key crypto anywhere in the design, so there is nothing for a future quantum computer to harvest today and break later. Against AES-256, Grover's algorithm still leaves a 128-bit security margin.

Attachments

Files are sliced into 256 KB chunks, each encrypted in your browser with the same key and bound to its exact position — the server stores opaque blobs it cannot read, reorder, or substitute. Filenames are encrypted too.

The boring, important parts

Everything is served over HTTPS with strict security headers (CSP with Trusted Types, HSTS preload, full cross-origin isolation). The pages load no external JavaScript, set no cookies, and run no analytics or tracking. Fragments never appear in our logs because they never arrive.

One request does leave our origin, and we'd rather name it than claim a round number: the badge at the bottom of this page is an image served by codecanary.org, a site the same people run. It publicly attests that the JavaScript you just loaded is the code we published — the check that matters most for browser-side encryption, since a silently swapped script could betray you and nothing else on this page would notice. Loading it tells that site your IP address and the time you opened the page. It is never told the link, the fragment, or anything about the secret.
